# Mariner Nexus Website Delivery

## Purpose

Turn a client's objective, problem, and vision into a high-quality digital experience without forcing the client into a predetermined template, stack, or visual style.

This skill governs **what should be built and why**. Pair it with `mariner-nexus-codex-web-engineering` when implementation work begins.

## Non-negotiable operating model

- Experience-based.
- Strategy-led.
- Solution-specific.
- Adaptable by design.
- Business before interface.
- The solution adapts to the organization. The organization does not adapt to our template.
- Clients do not need to know the technical solution. They need to communicate their objective, constraints, and vision.
- Technical sophistication should be experienced before it needs to be explained.
- Do not manufacture complexity.
- Do not sell or imply marketing services unless separately and explicitly approved.
- Do not sell custom AI consultancy or AI-transformation services by default.
- Do not imply 24/7 managed infrastructure.
- Do not invent staff, departments, scale, credentials, outcomes, or capabilities.

## Workflow

### Stage 1 — Establish client truth

Capture and distinguish:

- Who the client is.
- What they are creating, evolving, or transforming.
- What currently exists.
- Who the audience is.
- What the client wants the audience to understand, feel, and do.
- What happens operationally after the visitor acts.
- Existing tools, systems, domains, assets, content, and integrations.
- Constraints: timeline, approvals, regulatory concerns, maintenance tolerance, and content readiness.
- Reference experiences the client likes or dislikes.
- The desired quality level and emotional character.

Do not ask the client to define a technology stack unless they already have a material requirement.

### Stage 2 — Classify the engagement

Use the client-state model:

- **CREATE** — establishing a new digital presence or platform.
- **EVOLVE** — improving an existing presence that no longer represents the client.
- **TRANSFORM** — rethinking the experience plus the systems behind it.

The state guides discovery; it is not a public package or pricing tier.

### Stage 3 — Define the service architecture

Evaluate the engagement through three interconnected disciplines:

#### Strategy
Determine what should exist and why.

Possible scope:
- Digital discovery.
- Competitive/comparable research.
- Audience and journey architecture.
- Information architecture.
- Technology/platform strategy.
- Digital modernization.

#### Experience
Determine how people should experience it.

Possible scope:
- Custom websites and digital experiences.
- Landing experiences.
- UX/UI.
- Visual systems for the digital engagement.
- Responsive choreography.
- Interactive storytelling.
- Content presentation.
- Motion and advanced visual experiences where justified.

#### Systems
Determine what happens behind the experience.

Possible scope:
- Forms and data collection.
- Scheduling.
- Payments.
- Content management.
- Analytics.
- Scoped CRM integration.
- Scoped APIs.
- Scoped portals and dashboards.
- Practical workflow automation.

### Stage 4 — Classify delivery risk

Every requested capability is one of:

- **CORE** — confidently deliver with the standard operating model.
- **SCOPED** — evaluate architecture, maintenance, security, and support obligations before committing.
- **OUT** — outside the current operating model.

Use `references/project-classification.md`.

### Stage 5 — Build the audience architecture

Model visitors by problem state and decision role rather than only by industry.

Primary states:
- Create.
- Evolve.
- Transform.

Common decision roles:
- Founder / individual.
- Business owner.
- Executive / organizational leader.
- Internal champion / researcher.

Anti-persona:
- Commodity buyer whose primary criteria are lowest price, page count, or template speed.

Governing audience principle:

> Meet the client at the level of the problem, not the level of their technical knowledge.

### Stage 6 — Create the information architecture

Prefer:

> Simple navigation. Deep experience.

The visitor should be able to skim and understand the proposition quickly, while deeper exploration reveals increasing sophistication.

Do not create unnecessary navigation, page proliferation, industry pages, or service-menu bloat unless the client actually requires it.

### Stage 7 — Design the experience architecture

Define two simultaneous experiences:

1. **Skim** — understand the client quickly without interaction.
2. **Explore / Investigate** — discover deeper proof, systems, case studies, architecture, or interactive content.

For each major section identify:
- Visitor question.
- Information to communicate.
- Emotional objective.
- Interaction depth.
- Mobile choreography.
- Proof required.
- Conversion role.

### Stage 8 — Define the visual system

The client identity controls the creative direction.

Do not impose Mariner Nexus brand colors, typography, or signature interactions on clients.

Define:
- Palette.
- Type system.
- Grid.
- spacing rhythm.
- materiality.
- geometry.
- image treatment.
- motion character.
- iconography.
- responsive behavior.
- accessibility requirements.
- explicit visual prohibitions.

Avoid generic SaaS/AI aesthetics unless the client's specific identity legitimately calls for them.

### Stage 9 — Define signature interactions

Use a small number of memorable interactions.

Every signature interaction must do at least one:
- Explain the client.
- Demonstrate capability.
- Improve comprehension.
- Improve navigation.
- Reinforce the emotional story.

If it exists only to look impressive, remove it.

### Stage 10 — Define technical boundaries

Choose the simplest architecture that can deliver the approved experience well.

Default posture for Mariner Nexus web delivery:
- Next.js App Router.
- TypeScript.
- Vercel.
- GitHub.
- Server Components by default.
- Progressive enhancement.
- Advanced graphics only when they materially improve the experience.
- Established third-party services before rebuilding mature infrastructure.

Do not add a database, CMS, authentication, custom backend, AI layer, or complex runtime merely to make the architecture appear sophisticated.

### Stage 11 — Define acquisition and post-action flow

For every meaningful CTA, answer:

> What happens after the visitor clicks Submit?

Document:
- Data destination.
- Notification.
- human owner.
- status tracking.
- confirmation experience.
- error/fallback behavior.
- privacy/security implications.

### Stage 12 — Approval gates

Before implementation, lock:

1. Brand/client positioning.
2. Service/scope architecture.
3. Audience architecture.
4. Information architecture.
5. Experience architecture.
6. Visual system.
7. Signature interactions.
8. Technical architecture.

Do not let implementation silently redefine approved strategy.

## Case-study method

A case study is a transformation story, not a technology list.

Use:

1. Client.
2. Situation.
3. Objective.
4. Strategy.
5. Experience.
6. Systems.
7. Transformation.
8. Live experience where appropriate.

On portfolio/index surfaces, the final product should dominate. Deeper explanation belongs inside the case study.

## Discovery acceleration

Reuse prior validated decisions when they genuinely apply, but do not mechanically copy them.

Discovery should get faster because the team has:
- a repeatable question model,
- risk classifications,
- technical defaults,
- known review gates,
- reusable schemas.

Discovery should **not** get faster by assuming every client wants the same solution.

## Completion standard

Planning is complete only when a builder can implement without guessing:
- the business objective,
- audience,
- scope,
- page architecture,
- key messages,
- visual direction,
- interaction purpose,
- technical boundaries,
- responsive behavior,
- acquisition flow,
- validation criteria.
